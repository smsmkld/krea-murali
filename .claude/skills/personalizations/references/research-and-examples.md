## 1. Where to look, in order

Work down this list and stop as soon as you have one strong, dated fact. More research past that point does not improve the email.

1. **Careers page.** The single most underrated source. Open roles reveal what they are building, what is breaking, and where budget just got approved.
2. **Recent news and funding.** A dated event gives you a reason to be writing this week rather than any week.
3. **Their own content.** Podcast appearances, posts, webinars, newsletters. Their words, reusable verbatim.
4. **Pricing and product pages.** Structure reveals business model, deal size, and who the buyer is.
5. **LinkedIn, company page.** Headcount trend, recent hires, recent posts.
6. **LinkedIn, the individual.** Tenure, prior company, what they post about. Tenure matters: someone 90 days into a role is buying, someone eight years in is defending.
7. **Case studies and logos on their site.** Tells you who they serve, which tells you the pain their customers create for them.
8. **Review sites and forums.** What their customers complain about is often exactly the problem the client solves.

## 2. What each source is good for

| Source | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Careers page | Trigger + implied pain | Roles can sit posted for months, check the date |
| Funding news | Urgency, budget | Everyone emails after a raise, so the bridge has to be sharper than usual |
| Podcasts, posts | Their exact language | Do not quote something they said five years ago as if it is current |
| Pricing page | Deal size, buyer, model | Enterprise pages hide pricing, which is itself a signal |
| LinkedIn tenure | Buying posture | New hires are buyers, long tenures are defenders |
| Their case studies | Who they serve | Tells you their customer, not their pain, so bridge carefully |
| Review sites | Real complaints | Reviews skew negative, do not assume it is representative |

## 3. Worked examples

### Example A, careers page trigger

**Weak**
> "Hey Sarah, love what you're building at Northwind. Impressive growth over there."

Could be sent to any company. Says nothing. Gets deleted.

**Strong**
> "Sarah, saw you have three SDR roles open in Chicago and one sales ops role, all posted in the last month.
>
> Usually when a team scales outbound headcount that fast, the list building becomes the bottleneck before the reps do.
>
> We put together a short breakdown on how teams handle that at your stage. Want me to send it?"

Source: careers page, four roles, dated. The bridge names a real second-order problem instead of pitching. The CTA asks permission.

### Example B, their own words

**Weak**
> "Loved your recent podcast appearance, great insights!"

Proves you saw a thumbnail.

**Strong**
> "You mentioned on the Operators pod that you turned down two enterprise deals last quarter because onboarding could not absorb them.
>
> That constraint is usually the thing that caps growth long before pipeline does.
>
> We have a case study with a company that hit the same wall at roughly your size. Want it?"

Source: podcast, specific claim, paraphrased accurately. This is the strongest tier because it uses their own framing of their own problem.

### Example C, operational detail

**Weak**
> "Noticed you're in the logistics space."

Structural and generic.

**Strong**
> "Your pricing page runs per-seat up to 25 and then goes to 'contact us,' which usually means the 25-to-100 band is where deals slow down.
>
> That middle band is where most teams lose the most revenue to process, not to competitors.
>
> Put together a teardown on this exact gap. Want me to send it over?"

Source: pricing page structure. No news required, works on any company with a public pricing page, and demonstrates real thinking.

### Example D, when the research produced nothing

Do not write a line. Report it:

> FLAG, Marcus Webb, Delta Freight. No careers page, no news since 2021, no LinkedIn activity, no public pricing. Nothing verifiable to reference. Recommend manual research or move to the standard campaign.

## 4. Bridge patterns

The bridge is the sentence connecting their fact to the client's problem. Five patterns that work.

| Pattern | Shape | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Second-order problem | "When X happens, Y usually becomes the bottleneck" | You have a trigger and want to sound like an operator, not a vendor |
| Constraint naming | "That constraint usually caps growth before Z does" | They said the problem themselves |
| Pattern from peers | "Companies at your stage usually hit this at exactly this point" | You have proof from similar companies |
| Cost of the gap | "That gap is where most teams lose more than they think" | The pain is quiet and needs surfacing |
| Straight question | "Curious whether that has become a problem yet or not" | Low confidence in the read, and honesty converts better than a confident wrong guess |

The last one is underused. If you are guessing, ask instead of asserting. Prospects reply to a real question more often than to a confident misread.

## 5. Value CTA patterns

All of these ask permission rather than time.

- "We put together [asset] on exactly this. Want me to send it over?"
- "Have a case study with [similar company] that hit the same thing. Worth sending?"
- "Wrote up a short teardown on this. Want it?"
- "Happy to send over how [company] handled it, no strings. Interested?"

Notice what is absent. No calendar link. No "do you have 15 minutes." No "let me know a time that works." The moment a meeting ask appears, the email stops being a Dream 100 touch and becomes a normal cold email.

If the client has no asset to offer, the honest options are: request one, offer a specific insight in the email body itself, or move the account into a standard campaign. Do not substitute a meeting ask and call it Dream 100.

## 6. Handling a prospect with no public footprint

Common in trades, local services, and older privately held companies. Three real options:

1. **Personalize the company, not the person.** Location, size, service area, what their site says about who they serve.
2. **Go up or sideways.** A different decision maker at the same company may have a footprint the first one does not.
3. **Drop them from Dream 100.** A dream account with no research surface is not a Dream 100 candidate. Put them in a segment campaign where relevance carries the message and the effort matches the return.

What is never an option: writing a plausible-sounding line and hoping. On the accounts the client cares most about, a fabricated detail is the single most expensive mistake available.
